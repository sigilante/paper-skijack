\begin{abstract}
Following SKI-in-SKI, what sort of paper do you suppose we have in here?

A language paper, and the thesis is already sharper than the first paper's was at this stage, because a theorem draws it.

The result that carries it. 



What it is not, yet. Without self-hosting it is a design-and-implementation paper with an artifact, honest and complete, but its claim about quotation is illustrated rather than exercised. The moment the expander is written in the surface and compiles itself, the paper gains the result that closes the argument: the compiler, the thing that performs quotation, is an ordinary program on the far side of the line it enforces. That is the second milestone in the desiderata and it is what would lift the paper from ‹Programming› or a workshop (PEPM, the Scheme workshop) to something ICFP would read. Avon is a third paper, a systems one, and should stay out of this.

Its relationship to the first paper. The first says what an interpreter does at a new symbol. This one says how to make that the normal way to write interpreters, and what a language must give up, and need not, to do so over a calculus with no self-quotation. They are a pair, and the second should cite the first for the theorem and the counts, not restate them.

If you want, when step 5 lands I'll draft the contributions list and abstract in the SKIjack repo, so the language design gets written against a paper rather than the reverse.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Over a homoiconic calculus (Lisp, Nock) the metacircular tradition gets 
quotation for free and the language designer chooses where evaluation 
stages live. Over a non-homoiconic calculus that choice is taken away: 
Proposition 3.1 says no term can quote a live term, so the compile-time/
runtime line is fixed before the language is designed, and the two 
operations the calculus cannot do for itself, quote and recognize, go to 
the compiler and the runtime respectively.  \citet{Davis2027} presented
a metacircular evaluator for $SKI$ which is written and evaluated in the 
same combinator system.  This capability immediately suggests the 
construction of a battery of computational conveniences and 
abstractions within the same system; to wit, a language.  We present 
SKIjack (pronounced ``skyjack''), a closed-arm macro-like language
built in and  targeting the non-homoiconic $SKI$ calculus as its 
instruction set  architecture.  Every named thing compiles to a closed
term and every name is resolved before anything runs, so the language
has no runtime environment to address: bracket abstraction has already
closed what a subject-oriented calculus would have to carry.  SKIjack is evaluable on any $SKI$ 
interpreter as a virtual machine runtime.

The asymmetry that forces the staging line is older than either
calculus's programming languages, and naming it early makes the rest of
the design read as consequence rather than taste.  \citet{Bohm1968}
proved that two distinct $\beta\eta$-normal forms can always be told
apart from the outside: some context applies them to arguments and
recovers any two results we please.\footnote{Stated for the
$\lambda$-calculus and for $\beta\eta$-distinct normal forms; see also
\citet[\S10.4]{Barendregt1984}.  The probes this paper uses run on
Scott-encoded data under weak reduction, which is not that setting, so
we cite separability for the asymmetry it names and not as a
completeness result for the method.}  \citet{JayGivenWilson2011} proved
that no combinator can take another apart from the inside.  Both papers
of this pair live in the gap between those two results.  Reading a
compiled term by applying it to fresh markers and seeing which one comes
back is the outside; Proposition 3.1 is the inside; and a runtime jet is
permitted to factorise a live term precisely because it is the Turing
machine with the term on its tape, which is to say outside as well.
Quotation goes to the compiler and recognition to the runtime not
because that is convenient but because those are the only places the
operations exist.

The \texttt{whnfF} metacircular interpreter and \texttt{UQ} unquoter 
function presented in \citet{Davis2027} are the starting point for our 
work.  That paper discussed extensions to the $SKI$ calculus built on
higher layers of interpreters, introducing an \texttt{Err} error type,
a \texttt{Maybe} partiality type, and discussed implications such as
namespace value retrieval and other innovations.  By authoring a new
symbol at one site, the \texttt{whnfF} approach enables extension and 
modification of the combinator's semantics, permitting it to behave more
like a language than a mere combinator system.  This paper presents
SKIjack and three novel contributions for $SKI$ combinator research:
a two-level execution model with a kernel/userspace split; and a
namespace as a language feature:  a resolver is a core parameter, paths 
are a declared type compared structurally, and the namespace literal 
compiles to a resolver.


\section{Lineage}\label{sec:lineage}

A language whose semantics is combinators is not a new idea.
\citet{BohmGross1966} introduced the CUCH --- CUrry-CHurch --- a surface
for writing programs whose meaning is given by combinators, and SKIjack
is a late descendant of it.  What is new here is not the layering but
the constraint on it.  The CUCH was free to put its conveniences
wherever its designers liked; a language over a calculus that cannot
quote is not, and the interest of the exercise is in what that costs and
in what it does not.

Two further debts are specific.  A type declaration generates its
constructors and its case form by the schema \citet{BohmBerarducci1985}
gave for term algebras, in the Scott variant \citep{Scott1962,Jansen2013}
rather than the Church one: case analysis in place of a fold, which is
what an interpreter's step function wants and what fixes declaration
order as continuation order.  And the milestone that would close this
paper's argument --- an expander written in the surface that compiles
itself --- is the oldest move in compiler construction.
\citet{Bohm1954}, his 1951 dissertation at ETH Z\"urich, is generally
credited as the first compiler written in its own language
\citep{KnuthPardo1980}; seventy-five years later the move is the same
one, and here it would carry a further claim, since the compiler is the
component that performs quotation and self-hosting puts it on the far
side of the line it enforces.

\citet{BerarducciBohm1993} give a self-interpreter of the
$\lambda$-calculus having a normal form, which is the line of work the
artifact this language compiles belongs to.  Five of the results the
present paper leans on are B\"ohm's --- separability, the CUCH, the
term-algebra schema, the first self-compiling compiler, and a
self-interpreter with a normal form --- which is less a coincidence than
a reminder of how early these questions were asked.
